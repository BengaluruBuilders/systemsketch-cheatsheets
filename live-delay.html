Why “LIVE” is behind

Your phone told you “goal” before your stream showed it. The delay is there on purpose — and the obvious fix makes it worse.

The one idea
A live stream is usually sent as short clips. Your phone plays a few clips behind the newest one, so it always has a cushion of time while the next clip is still being made and sent.
How it works — in four steps
  1. The match is cut into short clips as it happens — often 6 to 10 seconds each.
  2. Each finished clip is added to a list that your phone keeps checking.
  3. Your phone starts about three clips behind the newest one, and plays older video while newer clips are still being made and sent.
  4. If each clip is 10 seconds long, that's at least 30 seconds behind the real match — before any other delay.

Standard: RFC 8216 (HLS), §6.3.3 and §6.2.1. Apple recommends 6-second clips (HLS Authoring Specification for Apple Devices §7.5).

The trap

Ask for a “more live” stream and the obvious fix is to start at the newest clip. Then nothing is waiting: if the next clip arrives a little late, the video freezes on a spinner.

Don't just jump to the newest clip to cut the delay; leave a cushion of time for late clips to arrive.
The four settings that control the delay
Setting What it does Make it smaller and…
Clip length The delay grows with it, because the phone waits a few clips back. Less delay, but more clips to make and fetch every minute.
Clips back at start The cushion. A typical phone starts about three clips behind the newest. Start closer than that and the video can freeze.
Piece length Low-delay streams send small pieces of a clip before the whole clip is ready. Pieces arrive sooner, so the phone can sit closer to live.
Pieces back at start The cushion, counted in pieces instead of whole clips. Same cushion count, far less time behind.

Rule-of-thumb: shrink the clips, not the cushion.

For engineers — the real names
Plain word HLS name What the spec says
Clip Media segment; EXT-X-TARGETDURATION Declares the maximum segment duration. RFC 8216 §6.2.1 calls 10 s typical; Apple recommends 6 s.
Clips back at start HOLD-BACK RFC 8216 §6.3.3: a clientSHOULD NOT start less than three target durations from the end of the playlist, because it can trigger playback stalls. It is a time, not a count of downloaded clips.
Piece Partial segment; EXT-X-PART Low-Latency HLS. Apple's guide uses 200 ms as an example, not a required value.
Pieces back at start PART-HOLD-BACK At least three part target durations (HLS Authoring Specification for Apple Devices §14.3).
Interview questions
  • QWhy doesn't the player start at the newest segment?
    A: The next segment may not be published yet. With nothing buffered, any late segment stalls playback. RFC 8216 §6.3.3 tells clients to stay at least three target durations back.
  • QIs “30 seconds behind” a rule?
    A: No. It is three target durations; with 10-second segments that is at least 30 s, with Apple's recommended 6 s it is at least 18 s, plus encoding and delivery time.
  • QA product manager wants the stream “more live”. What are the levers?
    A: Shorter segments, or Low-Latency HLS partial segments with a part hold-back. Moving the start point closer without shrinking the unit trades delay for stalls.
  • QWhat does a shorter segment cost?
    A: More segments, playlist updates and requests per minute for the encoder, CDN and player.
  • QWhy can a score alert beat the video?
    A: An alert is a tiny message sent once. The video has to be encoded, cut into segments, delivered and then played from behind a cushion.
